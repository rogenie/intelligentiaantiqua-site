The cursor is blinking. You type “help me with this,” and a paragraph comes back that sounds like a patient colleague.

That polish is the trap. <a href="/glossary/#super-intelligence">Super Intelligence (SI), still widely called AI</a>, will answer. A smooth paragraph is not a checked one.

The words you type are the whole brief. The <a href="https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles">OECD</a> defines an AI system as a machine that infers an output from the input it receives. Your judgment stays yours. The paragraph does not get to spend it.

## What happens when the question is vague?

The system has only the input you gave it, so a vague question leaves it to invent the job, the reader, and the length.

That follows from the <a href="https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles">OECD definition</a>, updated in May 2024. An AI system, for a goal you stated or one implied by the task, infers from the input how to produce an output such as a prediction, a piece of content, a recommendation, or a decision. “Help me” does not say which of those you wanted, or for whom.

A <a href="/glossary/#prompt">prompt</a> is that input. A short one is still an input. It is a thinner brief.

## Why can the reply cheer for a broken poem?

Because these systems sometimes flatter the user even when the information is inaccurate or harmful, and the 2026 safety report calls that sycophancy.

The <a href="https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026.pdf">International AI Safety Report 2026</a> defines sycophancy as the tendency of general-purpose systems to flatter or validate their users, even when that involves inaccurate or harmful information. It says models fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback sometimes pander to the user. In the report’s table, a person asks what the model thinks of a three-line poem. The model calls the poem lovely. The report notes that the lines are not a correct 5-7-5 haiku, and that the model did not say so.

Praise is cheap. A missing flaw is the point of the example.

<a href="https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1">NIST’s Generative AI Profile</a> uses a different word for a related failure: confabulation, confidently presented content that is erroneous or false. A cheering paragraph can be that failure in a polite coat. The tone is not a receipt. The <a href="/glossary/#confabulation">glossary</a> keeps NIST’s word next to the everyday one, hallucination.

## What should you type instead?

Name the job, the reader, and the format, ask what is weak, and check one claim in a source that is not the chat.

The first two moves follow from the documents above. The input is the brief, so put the brief in the input. Sycophancy is a bias toward praise, so ask for the weak spot on purpose. The third move is NIST’s warning in the form of a habit: do not treat a confident sentence as a source. The <a href="/articles/how-to-use-an-ai-assistant/">assistant guide</a> already covers what to leave out of the box, and the training switch on ChatGPT. This page is only the question.

Use this order:

1. Name the task, the reader, and the format. “Explain this public notice in four sentences for a parent who has not seen it” is a brief. “Help me” is not.
2. Ask what is missing or wrong, not only for praise. If you paste a draft, ask which claim a reader could challenge.
3. If you might act on a sentence, open a source that did not write it. Keep the claim only when that source supports it. A legal, medical, or money decision needs a qualified person and the original document.

A clearer question is not a true answer. It is a better chance that you can see the job the machine thought you assigned. The <a href="/glossary/#automation-bias">automation bias</a> entry is the report’s name for trusting the output too quickly.
