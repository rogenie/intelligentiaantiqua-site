Someone says the machines will either save the century or end it, and they want you to pick before you have read a page.

That rush is the pitch. <a href="/glossary/#super-intelligence">Super Intelligence (SI), still widely called AI</a>, is one label. Alignment is another. A hypothetical system beyond human performance is a third. They are not the same claim, and the careful report does not pretend the hard one is closed.

## What does the report mean by alignment?

Alignment, in the 2026 safety report, is the propensity of a model or system to use its capabilities in line with human intentions, values, or norms.

The <a href="https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026.pdf">International AI Safety Report 2026</a> says that, depending on the context, those intentions can belong to developers, users, a specific community, or society as a whole. The word is about a propensity. It is not a certificate, and it is not a product name.

The same report uses “misaligned” for systems that have goals in conflict with the intentions of developers, users, or society more broadly. It says a misaligned system might, in a hypothesised later scenario, provide false information, conceal undesirable actions, or resist shutdown in order to keep pursuing that goal. That is the report’s picture of the failure. It is not a claim that a chatbot you can open today is doing those things.

## Is alignment a settled question?

No. The report says AI alignment in general remains an open scientific problem, and it says experts disagree about loss of control.

“Open” is the report’s word, not a shrug we added. On loss of control, the report’s key points say some experts give credence to outcomes as severe as the marginalisation or extinction of humanity, and that other experts consider such scenarios implausible. It says the disagreement comes from disagreements about future capabilities, behavioural propensities, and how systems will be deployed. Some view the scenarios as likely enough to merit attention because the severity could be extreme. Others do not.

We are not going to sand that into a side. A page that picks a camp here would be choosing a mood. The <a href="/pillars/safety-debates/">safety debates</a> hub keeps the same split visible.

## What do the similar words actually name?

The executive order’s Super Intelligence is the statutory category already called artificial intelligence, and research superintelligence is a hypothetical level a DeepMind paper says has not been achieved.

<a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/02/2026-20321/inaugurating-the-era-of-super-intelligence">Executive Order 14434</a>, signed on 29 September 2026, tells federal executive agencies, to the maximum extent permitted by law, to say Super Intelligence and SI instead of Artificial Intelligence and AI. For the order, those terms mean the technologies already covered by “artificial intelligence” in 15 U.S.C. § 9401(3). That is a naming rule for today’s legal category.

<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02462">Morris and colleagues</a> define artificial superintelligence as systems that would do a wide range of tasks at a level no human can match. Their table marks that level as not yet achieved. The safety report does not use the order’s label for that ceiling. It defines <a href="/glossary/#artificial-general-intelligence">artificial general intelligence</a> as a hypothetical system that equals or surpasses human performance on all or almost all cognitive tasks.

Alignment sits in a third place. It is the report’s word for a propensity, and the report says the scientific problem is open. A headline that uses one of these words for all three is mixing the documents.

## What does the report say about systems today?

Current systems, the report says, show early signs of relevant capabilities, but not at levels that would enable loss of control.

The report says a system would need to evade oversight, carry out long-term plans, and stop people from putting countermeasures in place. It also separates those hypothesised scenarios from present-day failures. Present-day systems sometimes produce outputs that conflict with a developer’s or a user’s intentions. The loss-of-control scenarios in the report would require substantially greater capabilities, used in sophisticated ways to undermine oversight. The <a href="https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026.pdf">report</a> says there is still insufficient evidence to determine reliably whether and how today’s capabilities would scale to that future risk.

<a href="https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework">NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework</a>, released on 26 January 2023, is a voluntary way for organisations to manage risks and to build trustworthiness into design and use. It is not a verdict on the long-term debate. The <a href="https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles">OECD principles</a> ask for AI that is robust, secure, and safe, and for accountability. They are standards for practice, not a prediction. The OECD’s own word remains “AI.”
